Ste. Madeleine — From Ashes to Honour

A timeline of displacement and reclamation
for the Red River Métis community of Ste. Madeleine, Manitoba
Stage 1 — Thriving Community
Métis homesteaders settle Ste. Madeleine (1870s–1930s)
1870s – 1938 · Southwestern Manitoba, near Pumpkin Plain
The Métis of Ste. Madeleine arrived from the Red River Settlement, many after being forced out by Wolseley's Red River Expeditionary Force, and others returning from the 1885 Resistance. Over 250 Métis families established homesteads, built a log chapel (1913), a one-room school (1922), and maintained a tight-knit community bound by kinship, the French language, and the Catholic faith.

"They always considered Ste. Madeleine, with its log cabins, barns and outbuildings, to be their land base."

— Ste. Madeleine elders, interviewed 1986
Source: Metis Archive — "Historic Manitoba Métis Settlements" by Lawrence Barkwell, Louis Riel Institute, 2016
Stage 2 — Devastation
The Prairie Farm Rehabilitation Act displaces the community (1935–1938)
1935 – 1938 · PFRA designates Ste. Madeleine as community pasture
In the midst of the "Dirty Thirties", the Prairie Farm Rehabilitation Act designated Ste. Madeleine's land as community pasture to restore native grasslands. Only families who had paid all back taxes could claim compensation — a requirement most could not meet due to economic hardship. Those who remained were told to leave; their homes were burned, their dogs shot, and their church dismantled. The church bell and statues were sold; the logs were destined for a piggery.

"Their homes were burned, their dogs were shot, their church was to be dismantled and the logs sold to build a piggery."

— Metis Archive historical record

"Well then, in 1938, we were asked by the municipality to move out as they were going to build a community pasture."

— Joe Venne, Ste. Madeleine resident (b. 1906)
Source: Metis Archive — "Ste. Madeleine — Community Without a Town" (Zeilig & Zeilig, 1987); MMF Spotlight
Stage 3 — Resistance
Community saves the church from dismantling (1938)
1938 · Ste. Madeleine, Manitoba
Joe Venne and other community members armed themselves with rifles and confronted the crew sent to dismantle the Ste. Madeleine church. Their resistance succeeded — the church was saved. The family of Caroline and John Vermette was moved into the building to protect it. This act of defiance preserved the physical heart of the community.

"The plan to dismantle the church was foiled by Joe Venne and other community members armed themselves with rifles and confronted the crew sent to dismantle the church thus saving it."

— Metis Archive historical record
Source: Metis Archive — "Historic Manitoba Métis Settlements" by Lawrence Barkwell
Stage 4 — Remembrance
The land becomes pasture; only foundations and cemetery remain (1938–2024)
1938 – 2024 · Belliveau School foundations and cemetery persist
By 1938, the once-vibrant community had virtually vanished. Today, only the stone foundations of the Belliveau School remain, and the cemetery encircles the mound of grass where the church once stood. The wood from the schoolhouse was salvaged — a portion now constitutes the kitchen of the LeClerc home in Victor, Manitoba. The cemetery remains a sacred site; in 2016, the church bell was moved there from St. Hubert Mission.

"Today, all that remains of Ste. Madeleine are the stone foundations of the Belliveau School and the cemetery encircling the mound of grass where the church once stood."

— Metis Archive historical record
The displaced families were resettled in road allowance communities like The Corner (Fouillard Corner) and Selby Town near Binscarth. A 1958 census documented 170 Métis living in the Binscarth area. The names "Selby Town" and "Fouillard Corner" were given sarcastically, after the municipal officials responsible for the loss of Ste. Madeleine.
Source: Metis Archive — "Historic Manitoba Métis Settlements"; CBC (July 19, 2024)
Stage 5 — Reclamation
Métis regain recognition and land rights (2016–2024)
2016 · July 2024 · July–August 2026
The story of Ste. Madeleine has been documented in two important books: "Kanawayihtamaahk li taan paassii: Preserving Our Past—Ste. Madeleine, Manitoba" by Elder George Fleury (2016), and "Towards a Prairie Atonement" by Trevor Herriot and Elder Norman Fleury (2016). In July 2024, an MOU was signed transferring 100 acres of land at Ste. Madeelene back to the Manitoba Métis Federation, with a new cross raised at the cemetery. This weekend's CCAP community art project continues that reclamation — transforming memory into creative action.

"With the desire to protect native grasslands on community pastures, Herriot finds himself recruited into the work of reconciliation and conservation."

— From "Towards a Prairie Atonement" (2016)

"An MOU was signed transferring 100 acres of land back to the Manitoba Métis Federation. 'We've been working toward this for a long time,' said MMF President David Chartrand."

— CBC (July 19, 2024)
Sources: MMF Spotlight; CBC; APTN News; Barkwell, L. "Historic Manitoba Métis Settlements" (2016)
